SuperInnings

Baseball restructured into one continuous 27-out half per side, inside a full simulated world with its own stats, economy, and history.

Designer & sole engineer · 2026 · superinnin.gs

Cricket figured something out with T20: keep the sport, compress the structure, and a new strategic shape falls out of it. I’d been carrying around the baseball version of that thought for years: what happens if you keep all 27 outs but play them as a single arc? Each side bats once, straight through, until they record 27 outs. The starter takes the mound at out 0 and pitches as long as he can hold the line. The top of the order comes up six or seven times.

I’d built a fake computer sport before (Viperball), so I knew the order: write the rules down, build the engine, and let the results tell you whether the sport works. After months of iteration, it does. Pitcher fatigue accumulates across one continuous voyage, so a manager’s decisions matter. Lineup construction becomes a question of who gets the most plate appearances across the arc. And the players modern baseball has marginalized (the contact specialist, the workhorse, the junkballer, the knuckleballer) have jobs here. Every pitcher in this sport throws sidearm or submarine. That’s just the sport’s history.

The sport

A few rules do most of the work. Lineups run 12 deep, and every roster carries three jokers, tactical hitters a manager can drop into any spot in the order, once per trip through the lineup. Batters who make contact can choose to stay at the plate, trading a strike to advance their runners. The mechanic is borrowed from pesäpallo, and skilled hitters lean on it all game. A home run triggers the Walk-Back: the hitter returns to third as a live bonus runner who stays in play until he’s driven in or put out. A manager can also end his half early and bank the unused outs, a risk/reward call named Declared Seconds that buys a second trip through the lineup later in the game.

Pitching staffs are organized as a nautical crew, because the game is one continuous voyage: the Helms steers it out, the watch changes relieve him, the Bosun carries bulk, the Anchor holds late, the Pilot lands the final outs. Roles are derived from skill and relative to the staff: the same arm is a Helms on a thin team and a Skidder on a deep one. The pitch catalog runs 17 deep, and the sport’s structure brings back pitches baseball left behind: the knuckleball, the eephus, the spitter, the screwball.

Stats that fit the sport

A single at-bat can contain multiple hits here, so the whole stat suite is rebuilt. PAVG reads the way batting average used to; BAVG can climb past 1.000 for stay-heavy hitters, and the gap between them measures second-chance productivity. Innings pitched gives way to outs recorded and outs share. wERA weights runs by where in the 27-out arc they were surrendered; a run in the final stretch hurts more, because less arc remains to answer it. Decay measures how a pitcher’s strikeout rate erodes across the voyage, a skill this sport makes measurable. The analytics layer (linear weights, expected wOBA, run expectancy, Pythagorean projection) is re-derived from this league’s own data every cycle.

The world on top

The engine runs a league, and the league has an economy and a history. Thirty teams play 162-game seasons (configs run from 8 teams to a 56-team tiered pyramid with promotion and relegation). Players have salaries in guilders, traded on live auction markets. A motivation-driven trade engine pits rebuilders against win-now buyers at the deadline. Players age, get injured, develop, and retire; a youth pipeline and a 64-program college tier feed the pros; a 48-team youth World Cup and a Pro World Cup crown international champions. There’s a gated Hall of Fame, career leaderboards, end-of-season awards, named managers whose personalities change in-game outcomes, and a Fangraphs-style almanac for browsing it all.

Every ballpark has its own fence geometry, including pre-modern shapes. Weather mutates batted-ball physics game by game. A runtime tuning dashboard exposes every engine constant with era presets and a saved-environment library. There’s also a self-contained guide you can hand to an LLM to generate a whole play style from a plain-English description: “a deadball pitcher’s duel,” “a launch-angle circus.”

How it works

Every game is seed-deterministic: same seed, same roster state, same result. A stat-invariant suite runs nine assertions before any release, and it has caught every impossible-stat bug the project has shipped. Roughly 85 after-action reports in the repo document each system as it landed, with the reasoning, tradeoffs, and verification numbers.

The architecture

The core is Python with SQLite underneath and a Flask app on top. It simulates a full 30-team, 162-game season in minutes and runs anywhere Python runs. The web UI is one view of the engine. The same data exports as a static almanac, a newspaper-style box score, and Markdown built for LLM ingestion.

What you can do right now

  • Browse a season: standings, schedules, playoff brackets, awards.
  • Read a game: pitch-by-pitch play-by-play, newspaper box scores, per-game pitcher arcs.
  • Study the players: archetypes, career pages, scouting grades, salary valuations.
  • Follow the economy: auctions, trades, waivers, front-office personalities.
  • Explore the almanac: sortable heatmap leaderboards across every season, with full CSV/JSON exports.
  • Tune the universe: adjust engine constants, apply era presets, and build peer leagues with their own styles.
  • Read the methodology: the full stats reference, the design AARs, and the month-long project trajectory.
